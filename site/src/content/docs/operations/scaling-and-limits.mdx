## Parley runs as exactly one replica

This is not a recommendation. It is enforced, and a second instance will refuse
to start.

At boot, the process takes a Postgres session-scoped advisory lock
(`pg_try_advisory_lock`, key `0x7061726c6579`). If it cannot get the lock, it
logs that another instance holds it and exits non-zero.

The reason is the WebSocket hub. It lives in process memory, so two replicas
would each hold half the room: your vote would reach the three people who landed
on the same pod as you and nobody else. The room-code attempt throttle is
in-memory for the same reason — split across two replicas, the limit doubles.

Rather than let that fail quietly and confusingly at runtime, Parley refuses at
boot.

### What it costs

Deployments have a gap. The Kubernetes manifest uses `strategy: Recreate`
precisely because a rolling update would start a second pod that then exits on
the lock. So a deploy stops the old process and starts a new one, and for a few
seconds the board is stale and clients show their reconnect banner. That is the
honest price of an in-process hub, and it is why the manifest ships that way.

### The failover hazard

The lock is held by a connection, not by a row. If Postgres fails over, is
restored from a dump, or drops the connection, **the lock is gone** while the
original process may still be running and serving.

A new instance started at that moment will take the lock and boot happily,
alongside a zombie. That is exactly the split-brain the lock exists to prevent.
After any database failover or restore, confirm only one Parley process is
running before you rely on it.

Because the process sets no read or write timeout, **your reverse proxy is the
thing that will time out a WebSocket**. Parley pings every 25 seconds, which
resets an idle timer, so any proxy read timeout comfortably above that is safe —
75 seconds is the value used in the [reverse proxy](/operations/reverse-proxy/)
examples. A proxy defaulting to 60 seconds or less will cut sockets.

## How far does one replica go?

There is no published benchmark, and inventing one here would be dishonest.
What can be said from the design: state is in Postgres, broadcasts are
whole-session payloads sent to every connection in a room, and the hub holds one
goroutine pair per connection.

The scaling pressure is therefore **rooms × people × mutation rate**, and the
first thing to watch is the database, not the Go process. For a team, or several
teams, on modest hardware, this is not a concern. For an organisation-wide
deployment, multi-replica realtime is on the
[roadmap](/project/roadmap/) and is the thing that has to land first.
